WorldBox – God Simulator: Complete Game Design & Mechanics Breakdown
Research reference: October 10, 2026 | Android, iOS and PC




















13









WorldBox – God Simulator is a 2D, top-down, pixel-art god sandbox developed by Maxim Karpenko and published by Mako Mako on Android.
The player creates a world, introduces life, shapes ecosystems, watches civilizations develop, and intervenes using divine powers. It combines aspects of a civilization simulator, ecosystem sandbox, evolutionary simulation, and fantasy warfare simulator.
The defining characteristic is that the world continues developing without the player having to control it. Civilizations establish settlements, populations expand, kingdoms fight, creatures reproduce, environments change, and entire societies rise and collapse.
According to the official changelog, the current listed versions are Android 0.51.4 and Steam 0.51.2. The major 0.50 update introduced genetics, subspecies, religions, languages, families, possession, and substantially expanded simulation systems.

The four main gameplay pillars









1. Creation
Generate continents, paint biomes, raise mountains, spawn life and establish civilizations.











2. Observation
Watch populations grow, species evolve, cultures spread and settlements expand.











3. Intervention
Change traits, influence political relationships, trigger conflicts or protect favorite creatures.











4. Destruction
Unleash catastrophic powers, environmental hazards, supernatural threats and mass destruction.



Core gameplay loop
There is no fixed final victory. Players create their own experiments, stories and objectives.
Unlike a traditional strategy game, the central loop is not about winning battles or completing a campaign. It is about watching consequences unfold after changing the rules or conditions of the simulated world.

2. World creation and terrain generation




















14









WorldBox has two interconnected terrain systems: world generation and manual world editing.
2.1 Procedural world generation
Players can create worlds using automatic generation rather than constructing every island manually.
Important parameters and outcomes include:
- World size and overall landmass distribution.
- Continent-like landmasses, islands and archipelagos.
- Ocean basins and coastlines.
- Elevation differences, including hills and mountains.
- Forests, vegetation and biomes.
- Placement of natural resources.
- Procedural terrain variation.
The generated world serves as an initial state. The player can then modify practically any part of its landscape.

3.3 Biome spread
Biome boundaries are not always static.
The simulation can allow a biome to spread to neighboring eligible terrain. Growth is influenced by factors such as its intrinsic spread strength, the neighboring biome and the current world age.
For instance, under suitable conditions a jungle can expand into surrounding land. During the Ages of Ice and Despair, normal biome spreading is suppressed.
This creates gradual environmental changes even when the player is only observing.
3.4 Vegetation simulation
Plants and trees exist as resources within the world.
- Trees grow and can be harvested for wood.
- Bushes produce berries.
- Agricultural crops progress through growth stages.
- Rain can accelerate plant growth.
- Environmental heat or drought can kill vegetation.
- Fires can burn trees, crops and buildings.
- Some plants produce biome-specific foods or materials.
- Certain tree types survive extreme conditions better than others.
Vegetation is connected to civilization development because food, wood and other materials support settlement survival.
3.5 Water, weather and temperature
Water and heat interact with various systems:
- Rain extinguishes fires, affects lava and assists vegetation.
- Lightning can damage units or ignite fires.
- Snow and cold can affect movement, survival and farming.
- Extreme heat can cause fires and drought conditions.
- Acid can damage creatures, buildings and terrain.
- Lava spreads environmental danger and can transform into other terrain when cooled.
- Clouds, geysers and volcanoes introduce recurring environmental events.
Importantly, this is a rule-driven environmental simulation, not a scientifically complete fluid-dynamics or weather model. Many environmental interactions are discrete, tile-based effects.

4. Time simulation and world ages
WorldBox simulates the passing of months and years. Time advances while creatures make decisions, populations reproduce, civilizations expand and environmental processes continue.
Players can pause the simulation, change simulation speed and adjust the world-age cycle.
4.1 The ten ages

Hope

A generally favorable age for life and civilization growth.



Sun

Intense heat, drought, increased fire risk and vegetation stress.



Dark

Reduced visibility-related combat effectiveness and harsher crop conditions.



Tears

Persistent rain and wet conditions that suppress fires.



Moon

A mystical nighttime atmosphere with special trait interactions.



Chaos

A destabilizing age with stronger hostility and demonic transformation effects.



Wonders

An age associated with magical phenomena.



Ice

Freezing conditions and major environmental restrictions.



Ash

Ash-filled conditions associated with illness and decay.



Despair

A dangerous combination of cold, darkness and supernatural threats.



The ages change actual simulation rules rather than simply applying visual filters. For example, the Age of Sun can trigger drought damage, while the Age of Tears suppresses fires and the Age of Ice interferes with crop and biome growth.

5. Creatures, units and individual AI simulation
Every living entity is represented by a unit. Units are the foundation of almost everything happening in WorldBox.
A unit can be a human, animal, monster, magical creature or even an entity such as a ship.
5.1 Unit statistics
WorldBox tracks a surprisingly extensive set of individual characteristics.

Identity	Name, age, birth date, species, subspecies
Survival	Health, hunger, stamina, lifespan
Emotional	Happiness, relationships, temporary mental states
Combat	Damage, armor, attack speed, critical chance, movement speed
Magic	Mana and spell-related abilities
Leadership	Diplomacy, warfare, stewardship, intelligence
Progression	Level, experience, renown, kills
Reproduction	Children, offspring limits, parents and partners
Economy	Coins, carried resources and equipment
Society	Village, kingdom, army, family, clan, culture, language, religion
Physical	Size, mass, appearance and phenotype
History	Important events, relationships and accomplishments
These stats are connected. A ruler with high stewardship may manage more settlements effectively; warfare can improve a city's military capability; intelligence influences magical and political actions.

5.2 Unit AI behaviors
Individual units have decision-making systems covering multiple activities.
Survival behavior: Seeking food, consuming available resources, sleeping, recovering, avoiding hazards, fleeing from enemies and responding to injuries.
Social behavior: Finding partners, reproducing, forming friendships, interacting with other individuals and participating in families.
Civilization behavior: Gathering materials, constructing buildings, working on farms, joining armies, migrating, colonizing territory and using equipment.
Combat behavior: Searching for enemies, selecting attacks, using weapons, casting spells, defending settlements and following military captains.
Not every creature has all these behaviors. The capabilities available to a unit depend on its species, traits and memberships.

These differ from permanent traits. A status can be gained through combat, weather, food, magic or a temporary event.

6.3 Reproduction simulation
Reproduction is an actual gameplay system with conditions and consequences.
It includes:
- Sexual reproduction involving compatible adults.
- Hermaphroditic reproduction.
- Asexual reproduction through mechanisms such as fission or spores.
- Oviparity, with eggs that hatch.
- Viviparity, with pregnancy and birth.
- Special reproduction mechanisms associated with unusual subspecies.
- Inheritance of certain characteristics from parents.
- Reproductive limits related to age, nutrition and population.
For conventional reproduction, units may need sufficient nutrition, compatible partners, available housing and room within the population limit.
Newborns can inherit some parental traits, but random variation is possible.

Fandom



6.4 Population regulation
Population does not necessarily increase forever.
Growth depends on food availability, living conditions, fertility, species-specific reproductive rules, available settlement capacity and mortality.
A population may decline because of:
- Starvation or disease.
- Warfare and destruction.
- Old age.
- Dangerous wildlife.
- Environmental disasters.
- Infertility or unsuccessful reproduction.
- Excessive population pressure.
This creates a useful feedback loop: a growing kingdom needs more resources, more housing and more territory to sustain its citizens.

Trait effects are not always purely beneficial or harmful. For example, Giant increases size and health but reduces movement speed. Immortal prevents death from aging but can have social consequences.
7.2 How traits are acquired
Traits can come from random generation, reproduction, inherited characteristics, biome conditions, food, age, combat accomplishments, disease, mutations or direct player intervention.
Some traits are mutually exclusive. Others are contextual: they might trigger particular behaviors only under specific conditions.
7.3 Subspecies traits
These define characteristics shared by a subspecies rather than one individual.
Examples include:
- Reproductive method and reproductive strategy.
- Diet and food requirements.
- Expected lifespan and gestation.
- Environmental adaptations.
- Sapience and cognitive capabilities.
- Biological resource production.
- Natural skills and magical talents.
- Unique mutations and physical forms.
These are especially important because they change how future generations behave.
7.4 Trait editing
Players can inspect traits, unlock them through discovery or achievements, and modify traits using the relevant editors and powers.
Editing individual traits can leave a Scar of Divinity marker, while editing a subspecies can apply the GMO trait. Some achievement conditions specifically distinguish naturally developed units from player-modified ones.
This makes naturally occurring exceptional characters a separate collectible goal from deliberately created super-units.

8. Genetics, mutation, evolution and the Monolith




















5









This system is substantially more detailed than the genetics in many simple simulation games.
8.1 The gene editor
A subspecies has genetic structures organized into chromosomes with gene slots.
The genetics system includes:
- Different chromosome sizes.
- Multiple types of genes.
- Gene sequences and colored connection nodes.
- Stat bonuses.
- Synergy between compatible neighboring genes.
- Special gene behavior and mutation.
- Harmful genes and advantageous genes.
A well-connected gene can become synergized, increasing its effect. Genetic editing therefore has a spatial arrangement component in addition to choosing stat modifiers.

Fandom



8.2 Subspecies emergence
A species may contain multiple subspecies.
For example, two populations originating from the same animal species could develop different adaptations and biological characteristics.
New subspecies may arise through separation, mutation, evolution or special events.
Being in different subspecies does not automatically prevent reproduction between compatible members of the same underlying species.
8.3 The Monolith
The Monolith is a supernatural structure that can trigger evolutionary changes in nearby creatures.
It can mutate subspecies and, where a supported evolved form exists, transform animals into more advanced or humanoid forms.
For instance, a fox lineage can produce a sapient evolved form rather than remaining an ordinary animal population.
This creates a major gameplay possibility: the player can introduce a simple animal into the world and later watch an evolved population establish its own civilization.

Fandom



The game is not modeling Darwinian evolution with scientific biological accuracy. It uses fictional, rule-based mutation and transformation systems to create emergent outcomes.

9.3 Buildings and infrastructure
Civilizations can produce multiple building categories:
- Houses and upgraded residential buildings.
- Town halls and government structures.
- Farms and windmills.
- Mines and resource structures.
- Smithies and equipment-related infrastructure.
- Docks for naval operations.
- Watchtowers and defenses.
- Temples and religion-related buildings.
- Libraries and book storage.
- Roads, statues and decorative structures.
Architecture differs among civilization species and is influenced by cultural characteristics.
9.4 City planning AI
Cities make automated decisions about construction. They consider possible building types, available land, resources and their current situation.
Construction can be disrupted by hostile units, city capture and fires.
A village also claims new territory, allowing its boundaries to change over time.
9.5 Agriculture and famine
Farmers can cultivate crops to help sustain growing populations.
If growth fails or crops are destroyed, the food supply may shrink. This can affect reproduction and cause starvation.
Consequently, an environmental disaster can weaken a kingdom without directly attacking a single soldier.
9.6 Naval simulation
WorldBox has three main boat functions.
Vessel	Gameplay purpose
Fishing boat	Collects fish and returns them to the village
Trading boat	Travels between ports and generates gold
Transport boat	Moves settlers, soldiers and other units between landmasses; can fight
An important distinction: trading boats do not represent a fully modeled commodity-trading economy. The documented implementation primarily generates gold from port visits.
Transport ships make overseas colonization and invasion possible.

10. Kingdoms, politics and diplomacy




















11









A kingdom is the main political organization in WorldBox.
It can control multiple villages, appoint rulers, participate in wars, join alliances and expand across the map.
10.1 Political structure
Component	Responsibility
Kingdom	Controls territory and political relationships
King or queen	Leads the kingdom and influences decisions
Capital	Central settlement
Village leader	Manages an individual settlement
Clan	Represents influential lineages
Army captain	Directs military units
Alliance	Connects cooperating kingdoms
Plot	Represents a developing political or magical action
10.2 Kingdom expansion
Kingdoms may send settlers to new suitable land and create additional villages.
They can expand peacefully into unoccupied areas or acquire territory through conquest.
The geography of the map strongly influences their development. A kingdom surrounded by oceans may need ships to colonize distant islands, while a mainland kingdom can expand over connected land.
10.3 Diplomacy and kingdom opinions
Kingdoms maintain opinions of one another rather than treating every neighboring civilization as an automatic enemy.
Opinions are influenced by factors such as:
- Shared or differing species.
- Cultural and religious differences.
- Alliances and shared wars.
- Relative political or military power.
- Shared clans and ruler traits.
- Recent conflicts and periods of peace.
- World-age modifiers.
Political relationships can change as these conditions change.

Worldbox Wiki



10.4 Alliances
Kingdoms can form and join alliances, cooperate during wars, and dissolve political relationships.
Alliances can increase the scale of conflicts because a war involving one kingdom may pull in its allies.
10.5 Rebellions, loyalty and succession
A kingdom's political stability depends partly on the loyalty of its settlements.
A village may rebel and form a separate kingdom. Leadership changes can also alter political relationships.
WorldBox tracks former kings, heirs, founders and significant events, helping turn ordinary simulation outcomes into a persistent history.
10.6 Plots and political actions
Plots are actions planned by eligible characters, usually leaders or influential members of society.
Representative plots include:
Plot	Outcome
War planning	Initiates a conflict
Alliance formation	Establishes cooperation between kingdoms
Rebellion	Separates territory from an existing kingdom
Political or cultural split	Creates a new social or political division when requirements are met
New book	Produces a book with potential social or stat effects
Magical rites	Triggers religion-dependent events
Ascension	Can transform a lineage into a new subspecies
Plots depend on eligibility, resources, traits and political circumstances, so they do not necessarily complete successfully.

11.1 Cultures
Cultures influence social and functional characteristics of civilizations.
Culture traits can influence architecture, building patterns, military recruitment, the treatment of different ages, succession and weapon preferences.

11.4 Clans
Clans represent influential lineages and play roles in leadership and political power.
They have their own traits and can affect succession, individual protections or special events.
Some clan characteristics are connected to magical ascension and hereditary identity.
11.5 Families
Families are formed through reproduction and contain parents and children.
The game also tracks genealogy and family origins.
Children can eventually leave the original family structure by reproducing and establishing their own family.
Family structures are not a perfectly realistic genealogical simulation, but they create persistent relationships and historical connections.

11.6 Books and knowledge
WorldBox also includes a book system.
Eligible individuals can write books, and others may read them.
Books may grant experience or bonuses and influence the transmission of cultural, religious or linguistic traits.
Books are mechanically represented objects: players cannot open and read a procedurally written novel inside the game.

12. Warfare and military simulation




















9









Warfare is one of the game's most visible emergent systems.
12.1 Military organization
An army is made up of units recruited from its settlement.
Armies have captains who guide their movement and help coordinate soldiers.
The 0.51 ArmyBox update made armies individually trackable social objects, with their own information panels and history.
12.2 Combat behavior
Soldiers can use melee and ranged weapons and, when eligible, supernatural abilities.
Combat involves:
- Damage, armor and attack speed.
- Movement and enemy targeting.
- Critical strikes.
- Weapon and equipment modifiers.
- Unit experience and combat traits.
- Special attacks and spells.
- Environmental damage.
- Retreating or fleeing behavior.
- Military coordination through captains.
12.3 Weapons and equipment
Units can use categories such as swords, axes, hammers, spears, bows and magical staffs.
Items can be crafted from different materials and may receive modifiers that improve their performance.
Equipment slots include:
Equipment	Purpose
Weapon	Offensive ability
Helmet	Protection and bonuses
Armor	Damage resistance
Boots	Protection or other bonuses
Ring	Accessory bonuses
Amulet	Additional bonuses
12.4 Siege and territorial conquest
During warfare, armies move toward enemy territory and fight defending units.
The result may be the capture, destruction or loss of settlements.
Watchtowers can attack intruders, and walls influence access to defended areas.
12.5 Naval warfare
Ships can move soldiers across the sea and, depending on their type, attack enemies.
This introduces amphibious invasions and makes geography politically meaningful.
12.6 Types of war
The war system includes naturally initiated conquest and conflicts forced by divine powers.
For example, Spite and Whisper of War allow the player to influence conflict directly.
Players can therefore observe politics evolving naturally or deliberately cause wars to test the survival of their favorite kingdoms.

13. Diseases, infections, monsters and special threats
The game contains dangers beyond ordinary armies.

Plague	Disease can spread through susceptible populations
Zombie infection	Can transform victims into undead forms
Mushroom infection	Can produce hostile mushroom-associated transformations
Tumor infection	Can transform creatures or spread unnatural life
Necromancers	Use undead and magical abilities

An important design principle here is cascading failure.
A plague may reduce a city's population. That population decline can weaken its army, which can make it easier to conquer. A conquered kingdom may then lose settlements and political stability.
No individual system has to explicitly script the entire catastrophe. It emerges through interconnected rules.

15. World laws: the sandbox rule system
World laws are global switches that determine which simulation behaviors are allowed.
Examples include:
World law	Function
Hunger	Controls hunger-related survival
Old age	Determines whether aging can kill units
Animal spawning	Enables natural animal generation
Kingdom expansion	Allows kingdoms to establish new settlements
Armies	Enables army formation
Angry Villagers	Changes civilian participation in warfare
Magic Rites	Enables religion-based magical plots
Glitched Noosphere	Makes cultural, linguistic, clan and religious trait generation more unpredictable
Mutant Box	Introduces additional random subspecies traits
Gene Spaghetti	Randomizes aspects of genetic generation
Gaia's Covenant	Prevents various types of terrain destruction
Terramorphing	Allows certain structures to spread their characteristic biomes
Evolution Events	Enables special evolution-related events
Several laws are designed specifically for unusual, chaotic experiments, not realistic world behavior.
This is a powerful sandbox feature because players can change the underlying rules rather than merely changing units or terrain.

16. User interface, inspection and player controls
WorldBox uses a map-first interface: the simulated world fills most of the screen, while tools and inspection controls are grouped into compact panels.

Main interface features
World interaction: Pan and zoom, select creatures, paint terrain, place objects, and apply powers using adjustable brush sizes.
Inspection: Open detailed information windows for creatures, villages, kingdoms, religions, families, cultures, languages, armies and subspecies.
Map overlays: Display political borders, social distributions, armies and other world data without changing the underlying simulation.
Tracking: Favorite important units, follow selected entities and inspect their histories.
Time control: Pause, resume and accelerate simulation time.
World management: Create, save, load and manage worlds, alongside autosave options.
Statistics: Inspect population counts, historical outcomes and graphs for different world entities.

17. Art direction — detailed analysis




















16









WorldBox's visuals work because they use a consistent, relatively simple language across a large simulation.
17.1 Overall style
Element	Art direction
Perspective	Direct top-down and slightly interpreted top-down sprites
Visual language	Fantasy pixel art with cartoon-like readability
Environment	Small repeating terrain textures and larger decorative objects
Characters	Tiny animated sprites with distinctive silhouettes
Buildings	Stylized roofs, walls and faction-specific variations
Colors	Bright, distinguishable palettes with strong biome identities
Lighting	Primarily stylized color and effect-based presentation
Combat effects	Compact but highly visible particles and projectiles
UI	Dark, game-themed panels with bright icons and labels
17.2 Terrain artwork
The underlying ground uses repeating pixel-art patterns.
Small variations prevent large regions from looking completely uniform.
Terrain boundaries use transitional shapes and textures so beaches, grass, soil, water and cliffs are distinguishable even when zoomed out.
17.3 Character artwork
Characters are deliberately tiny.
Their visual design prioritizes identification over anatomical realism.
A sprite typically communicates identity through its head, color, silhouette, weapons and a small number of animated poses.
Different subspecies and physical mutations can alter appearance.
17.4 Buildings
Buildings use simplified geometry, colorful roofs, wooden or stone structures and readable silhouettes.
Villages visually become denser as populations grow and infrastructure develops.
This is important because the state of a civilization can be understood from its appearance, not only from statistics.
17.5 Animation
Common animation needs include idle motion, walking, attacks, death, spellcasting, fire, particle effects and environmental changes.
The presentation favors compact animations with strong readability instead of expensive, full-frame animation for every entity.
17.6 Effects and visual feedback
WorldBox makes major events immediately noticeable with:
- Explosion flashes and shockwaves.
- Fire, smoke and debris particles.
- Magic projectiles and glowing effects.
- Lightning strikes.
- Weather particles.
- Damage and impact animations.
- Status indicators.
- Destruction of terrain and buildings.
The effects are intentionally more dramatic than the tiny character sprites. This produces strong visual feedback during disasters and large battles.
17.7 Suggested style specification for a similar game
This is a design interpretation, not a claim about WorldBox's original production files.
Asset	Practical direction
Base ground tile	16×16 pixel working grid
Small characters	Approximately 16×16 to 24×24 sprite canvases
Large creatures	Variable dimensions based on silhouette
Buildings	Modular 32×32 and larger pieces
Palette	Restricted color families per biome
Animations	Approximately 4–8 frames for routine actions where suitable
Rendering	Pixel snapping and nearest-neighbor filtering
Camera	Orthographic-style top-down camera
VFX	Pixel particles, sprite animations and lightweight shader effects
The game's art direction is especially suitable for large populations because its simple geometry and compact animation vocabulary make it easier to maintain visual consistency.

19. Why the simulation is compelling
WorldBox's strength is not simply the number of features. It is the interaction between them.
Here is one hypothetical scenario illustrating how its rules can combine.
An emergent history: The Fall of the Northern Empire
1. A prosperous human settlement grows near farmland, forests and a river.
2. Its population increases, producing new villages and eventually a large kingdom.
3. The kingdom builds an army and expands into territory controlled by another civilization.
4. A conflict begins, weakening both populations and destroying agricultural areas.
5. The world enters a harsh age, reducing crop production further.
6. Starvation lowers the surviving population and weakens military recruitment.
7. A neighboring power attacks while the kingdom is vulnerable.
8. The capital falls, the ruling clan loses power and the remaining settlements change political control.
9. Centuries later, new societies inhabit the old empire's former territory.
This is an illustrative sequence of possible interactions, not a scripted campaign or guaranteed sequence of events.
The appeal lies in three things: unpredictable outcomes, the ability to inspect the individual histories behind those outcomes, and divine intervention that lets the player create an entirely different result.

20. Game-system architecture: how the mechanics connect
For a game designer or developer, it is useful to think of WorldBox as a collection of linked simulation subsystems.
Conceptual architecture showing the major relationships between simulation systems.
A well-structured implementation would separate responsibilities like this:
System	Main responsibility
World manager	Time, world state, simulation scheduling
Terrain system	Tile properties, elevation and editing
Biome system	Spreading, flora, resources, environmental modifiers
Entity system	Individual units and their data
AI system	Survival, work, combat and decision-making
Genetic system	Species, subspecies, genes and mutations
Population system	Reproduction, aging, mortality and migration
Settlement system	Buildings, territory, housing and resource storage
Economy system	Collection, crafting and resource consumption
Political system	Kingdoms, loyalty, alliances and leadership
Social system	Families, clans, cultures, languages and religions
Combat system	Damage, equipment, battles and armies
Disaster system	Weather, explosions, infections and special events
History system	Event logging, graphs and tracked entities
God-power system	Player-triggered changes and interventions
UI system	Inspectors, overlays, brushes, controls and saves
This is a recommended architectural decomposition for studying or building a similar game, not a description of WorldBox's proprietary source code.
21. What to prioritize when building a game inspired by WorldBox
Trying to reproduce all these systems at once would be a very large project.
For a Godot-based 2D god simulator, I would divide development into the following milestones:
Phase	Deliverable	Priority
1	Tile-based world generator, oceans, islands and camera	Essential
2	Editable terrain, 4–6 biomes and vegetation	Essential
3	Creature movement, survival AI and reproduction	Essential
4	Villages, simple buildings, farming and gathering	Essential
5	Kingdoms, borders, expansion and basic diplomacy	Essential
6	Soldiers, wars, conquest and defense	High
7	Individual traits, inheritance and subspecies	High
8	Weather, disasters, diseases and divine powers	High
9	Cultures, families, clans, religions and languages	Advanced
10	Evolution, genealogy, deep histories and advanced editors	Advanced
The most important technical problem will be simulation scalability. Thousands of independent units making decisions, finding paths, searching for food and fighting simultaneously can become far more expensive than rendering simple pixel-art sprites.
For Godot 4.4, I would favor data-oriented simulation managers, chunk-based world processing, staggered AI updates, shared navigation data and lightweight visual nodes rather than giving every creature an expensive _process() loop.

Final assessment
WorldBox succeeds because it combines three different layers of interaction:
The microscopic layer: Individual creatures with genetics, health, hunger, personalities, relationships and behavior.
The societal layer: Villages, armies, kingdoms, cultures, religions, families and political history.
The planetary layer: Terrain, biomes, climate-like ages, disasters, ecological change and divine interventions.
The player can interact with any of these layers and observe effects on the others.
For a developer studying the game, the essential insight is that WorldBox is not one extraordinarily complicated AI. It is a large collection of comparatively understandable simulation rules connected in ways that produce surprising outcomes.
